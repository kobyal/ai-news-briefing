## 1,130 tanks and an area the size of Washington

In mid-September John Wang published a post on the engineering behind the US Strategic Petroleum Reserve. He starts from the requirements, not the solution. The reserve has to store 714 million barrels for decades and protect them from attack. It has to release them fast in a crisis. And maintenance can't cost a fortune.

The naive solution is to take what the industry already does and scale it up. A standard floating-roof tank tops out around 50 feet high and 300 feet across, and holds about 630,000 barrels. For the full reserve you need 1,130 of them, roughly 45,000 acres. That's about the size of Washington, D.C. Bonus: you now have a thousand-plus flammable targets, each with a known weak point where the floating roof meets the wall.

The real solution is salt caverns underground. Boring and good.

So why oil on an AI blog? Because this week the word that showed up most in the headlines we collected wasn't prompt or context. It was just "the engineering". "The engineering behind", "the engineering around us", "The Engineering State". No prefix.

## Drop the prefix

Nobody coined "the engineering", so this isn't a term in the usual sense. But it's exactly what's left when you strip the prefix off the last two years of terms: prompt engineering, context engineering, harness engineering. Each one is basically the same old discipline with an LLM in the middle.

What it isn't: a job title. Udacity already has a full career guide page for "Agentic AI Engineer", with a skills list and a "why the role is growing" section. When a term lands in a job title before it lands in a paper, we get suspicious.

There's also a nice argument from a completely different field. In Breakneck, Dan Wang argues that China is an "engineering state" and the US is a society of lawyers. He told Tyler Cowen the US should be 20% more engineering-minded and China 50% more legalistic. A review published in August on One Thousand Means argues this is a flattering abstraction, not an explanation, and one of its sections is titled "the difference between an and the". That's the point. "An engineering" is something you do. "The engineering" is a word that sounds good in a headline.

## Requirements before the model

What the oil post actually teaches is an order of operations, and it's the order most teams building agents skip:

- **Requirements first, in writing.** What the system must do, and what it must never do.
- **Then the naive solution, with numbers.** How many tokens, how long, what a run costs. 1,130 tanks is a number that kills an idea fast.
- **Then ask what can be deterministic.** Anything a rule can solve, a rule solves.
- **Only then add a model, where you actually need judgment.**

Colab Software, which builds agents for product engineering, put it well: a real agent produces outputs that are "repeatable, testable, and predictable", it's specific to one workflow, and it has defined triggers and handoff points back to the engineer. None of those is a prompt. They're all decisions about the system around it.

## A PM, an ESP32 and Claude

The best example this week came from Show HN. A dad, a product manager by trade, built a private intercom for his 11-year-old son and four friends: tap a name, the friend's unit rings, you talk. No accounts, no feed, no search. Six ESP32-S3 boards with 2.8-inch touchscreens, mics and speakers, about 250 Canadian dollars in total. It boots in under two seconds because there's no OS.

He had never written firmware or touched I2S. He did all of it with Claude, including Opus, as an engineering partner. And he's refreshingly honest about it: he didn't build it, he assembled it.

But look at what he did do. The roster lives on his own server, and each unit only opens outbound connections to a relay on a VPS he owns. A kid who isn't on the list can't ring, because the device has no way to represent them at all. That's not a filter. It's a constraint designed in from the start. The model wrote the audio code. The decisions that make it safe for kids were his. That's the engineering.

## In our pipeline

The aibriefing.dev pipeline has been running in production since May 2026: a graph of about 10 agents, a merger step, and a QA evaluator that runs after publish and flags P0s. Our worst bugs weren't wrong answers. They were silent failures: a run that reports success and publishes nothing.

What fixed them? Not a better model. Our wrapper around `claude -p` retries transient failures and repairs broken JSON. A small, boring loop, and it closed three production incidents. Every place we swapped an LLM call for a rule came out cheaper and more reliable. And when we copied logic between agents instead of centralizing it in `shared/`, we got real incidents, because a fix landed in one copy and not the other.

In short, the oil reserve, the intercom and our pipeline never got a cool name. They have no prompt engineering. They have requirements, constraints and testing. And the model, when it's there at all, is one component inside a system where someone decided how it fails.
