Three. That's how many things Anthropic says you need before you start prompt engineering: a clear definition of success, a way to test it empirically, and a first draft of a prompt. Two of the three are text the model will never read.

That's where things stand in late 2026, in one line. Most of the work has moved out of the prompt. What's left inside is smaller, and it matters more.

This is part 1 of 3. Here we cover what still counts as prompt engineering, what moved to context and the harness, and the five things that still move results. How a production system prompt is structured is part 2. How you know a change actually helped is part 3.

## The 2023 prediction that half came true

Nobody coined the term. It just stuck. It happened around GPT-3, in 2020–2021, when people found that the same model answered very differently depending on the wording. By 2023 it was mostly a bag of incantations: “think step by step”, a few examples, “you are a world-class expert in X”.

In 2023 Yiblet wrote that this era would be “transient”, a few years and done. In May 2026 he revisited the post and said he was only half right. The “coax a chatbot into a better answer” version really has nearly disappeared. What remains, in his words, is making AI systems reliable “when they have context, tools, memory, permissions, and real consequences”.

Basically, the incantations died because models learned to do them on their own. Reasoning models think step by step without being asked. What didn't die is the need to explain what you actually want.


## What moved from the prompt to the harness

Take a prompt from 2023 and go through it line by line. Half of those lines are now written in code.

**Format.** We used to write “return JSON only, no explanations” and hope for the best. Now OpenAI has Structured Outputs. Their docs say outright that `output` “often has more than one item”, and that it isn't safe to assume the text is at `output[0].content[0].text`. In day-to-day terms: format and parsing are the harness's job. Wording won't fix them.

**Context.** In the Claude Code guide, Anthropic writes that almost every best practice follows from one constraint. The context window fills up fast, and performance drops as it fills. A single debugging session can burn tens of thousands of tokens. What goes into context, when to clear it and what to summarize is context engineering. You can't solve it with one more line in the prompt.

**Verification.** The same guide says to give Claude something that returns pass or fail: tests, an exit code, a linter, a diff against a fixture. That loop lives in the harness.

## What stays in the prompt

What does stay is the one thing no tool can guess for you: intent.

In March 2026 Michael Heap wrote about trello-cli, a CLI tool he maintains. He asked an agent to “Add support for showing card details” and got a command that showed the title, description and labels. No due date, which is exactly the field he cares about most, and no checklists. So he rewrote the request. A `card:show` command with `--board` and `--list` flags, a list of fields to display, skip the header for an empty field, and “+ X more” when a checklist has more than 10 items. This time it worked, and a task that would have taken him an hour was done in a few minutes.

His line: “Nothing about the model changed. The only thing that changed was the context.” I'd put it a little differently. What changed is that he stopped assuming the model knew what was in his head.

Yiblet adds the agent angle. When a chatbot misunderstands you, you get a bad answer. When an agent misunderstands, it can update a DB, message a customer, or confidently skip an important step. So the prompt also has to describe the boundaries: what not to do, when to ask, and when to stop.

## So is it engineering or not

In March, the-infrastructure-mindset published a piece called “Prompt Engineering Is Not”. The author has four decades of infrastructure behind him. He went looking for the discipline: formal methods, a testing framework, measurement. What he found, he says, was “creative writing advice with a technical veneer” and posts that say “be specific” and “iterate”.

For most of what's online, he's right. But go back to Anthropic's three prerequisites. Two of them are exactly what he was looking for: a definition of success and an empirical test. And the same page has another sentence most prompt guides skip:

> Not every success criteria or failing eval is best solved by prompt engineering. For example, you can sometimes improve latency and cost more easily by selecting a different model.
> — Anthropic, Prompt engineering overview

In short, prompt engineering without an eval really is creative writing. With an eval, it's a change you can measure. scapegoat.dev put it best. When a model fails, there's a finite set of reasons: I worded it wrong, the model can't actually do this, bad luck, or something else is broken, like a tool or the vendor's harness. Each of them can be diagnosed.

## What we saw in our own pipeline

At aibriefing.dev we run a graph of about 10 agents every day, in production since May 2026. Looking back at the incidents, almost none were fixed by changing the wording.

Our wrapper around `claude -p` retries transient errors and repairs broken JSON. That small loop closed three production incidents. Everywhere we replaced an LLM call with a deterministic rule, the pipeline got cheaper and more stable. Translation and classification run on cheap models, and the heavy writing runs on Claude. Those are all system decisions, not prompt decisions.

What did work inside the prompt? For Hebrew translation, the thing that moved quality most was pairs of bad sentence vs. good sentence in the system prompt. It beat every “write naturally” we tried before it.

> **My take:** Most of the time people burn on “improving the prompt” should have gone into an eval. I've rewritten prompts after incidents plenty of times, and the problem was almost always in what the model saw, not in what I told it.

## Five things that still move results

OK, so what do you do on Monday. These are the five things that, for us, still change results when they're written into the prompt:

1. **Define “done”.** Per the Claude Code docs, the model stops when the work “looks” done. Write down what counts as done: which fields, which test has to pass, what length.
2. **Write the whole intent, not just the task.** Heap's first version was one sentence. His second was five. Every assumption that stays in your head and doesn't make it into the text, the model will fill in on its own.
3. **Boundaries and stopping points.** What's forbidden, when to ask before continuing, when to stop and report. In an agent, that's the difference between a bad answer and a changed DB.
4. **One real example, preferably bad vs. good.** Describing a style works worse than a pair of sentences. How to build these, and when few-shot is worth the cost, is in part 2.
5. **Tell it how to check itself.** “Run the tests before you finish” is one line that connects the prompt to the harness.

Notice what's not on the list: any wording trick. No “you are a world-class expert”, no CAPS, no promised tip. And even with all five, if you have no way to measure whether any of them helped, you're still pulling a slot machine lever.
