## 16,150 decisions for $1.65

Christian Mathiesen from Frigade hooked Jev up to Pokémon Red and let it play until the Hall of Fame. The run took 37 hours and 40 minutes. It made 16,150 decisions on about 39.2 million input tokens, and the total cost came to roughly $1.65. Median time per decision was 0.4 seconds. Along the way the whole team wiped 16 times, and it took 15 attempts at the Elite Four.

For comparison, TypeSafe's homepage shows one call to a regular LLM: 8.566 seconds and $0.01388. The same task on Jev: 0.114 seconds and $0.000081. Those are their numbers on their workflows, but the order of magnitude is clear.

Jev shipped on September 15, 2026 from TypeSafe AI. The founder is Diogo Almeida, who worked at OpenAI on the methods that made models good at instruction following. That research eventually became ChatGPT. After two years in stealth he came back with one question: we've had superhuman chat models for years, so where's all the automation?

## What it returns, and what it doesn't

Jev doesn't generate text. At all. You send it a state, as text or JSON, plus a list of questions, each with possible answers you define up front. For each question it returns a typed decision and a probability. TypeSafe calls this category a System One Model.

An example from Ollaya: a customer writes "third time this year you charged me twice, refund me today or I'm leaving". Five questions, answered in 87ms on an RTX 4090: intent=refund at 0.91, is_urgent=yes at 0.92, churn_risk=yes at 0.99. No paragraph, no "I understand your frustration". Just values your code can act on.

What it isn't: it's not JSON mode. With structured outputs the model still writes a string token by token, and you parse, validate and hope. With Jev the answer space is closed in advance, so a type error can't happen. It's also not a small chat LLM. It won't write your emails.

What about "Zero Hallucinations"? Technically true, since it can't invent a value you didn't define. But it can absolutely pick the wrong value with full confidence. That's not a hallucination, it's a mistake. In production, the name doesn't matter much.

## What's inside

TypeSafe describes three parts: a new architecture, a parallel sampler, and a training method they call RLCD, Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions. RLHF trains a model on what human raters prefer. RLCD trains it so the probability it reports matches reality. If it says 0.9, it should be right about 9 times out of 10.

Arcturus Labs bets that under the hood it's fairly close to a regular LLM. Their argument: most of the first clones are LLM-based, and Latent Space counted six of them. The mechanism, as they describe it:

- Run one forward pass over the state and the questions.
- Take the next-token distribution.
- Look only at the tokens that represent the options.
- Normalize them into probabilities and map them to the type you defined.

That's why it's so fast: no generation, no loop over tokens.

The part that actually matters to you is thresholds. Above 0.95 your code acts on its own; below that, the decision goes to review. That only works with calibration. Without it, the number is decoration.

## Jev in 25 lines of Python

On September 22, nobodywho published a post with a pointed title: Jev in 25 lines of Python. They took Qwen3-0.6B, a tiny open model, and ran it through llama.cpp. They asked whether an email was Legitimate, Spam or Phishing, and took the logits for the three letters. Result: Phishing at 0.885.

```python
# pip install huggingface-hub llama-cpp-python numpy
import numpy as np
from llama_cpp import Llama

model = Llama.from_pretrained(repo_id="Qwen/Qwen3-0.6B-GGUF",
    filename="Qwen3-0.6B-Q8_0.gguf", n_ctx=512, logits_all=True, verbose=False)

labels, choices = ["A", "B", "C"], ["Legitimate", "Spam", "Phishing"]
email = "Payroll asks for your password on a non-company sign-in page."
options = "\n".join(f"{l}. {c}" for l, c in zip(labels, choices))
prompt = (f"<|im_start|>system\nChoose one option.<|im_end|>\n"
          f"<|im_start|>user\nEmail: {email}\n\n{options}<|im_end|>\n"
          f"<|im_start|>assistant\n<think>\n\n</think>\n\n")
model.eval(model.tokenize(prompt.encode(), add_bos=False, special=True))

logits = model.scores[model.n_tokens - 1]
ids = [model.tokenize(l.encode(), add_bos=False)[0] for l in labels]
x = np.array([logits[i] for i in ids])
p = np.exp(x - np.logaddexp.reduce(x))
print(dict(zip(choices, p.round(3))))
```

They're half right. The mechanism is old. Arcturus points out that OpenAI has used LLMs as implicit classifiers for years; it just never packaged it as a product. What the 25 lines don't give you is calibration. When Qwen says 0.885, you have no idea whether that's really 88%. That's exactly what RLCD is for. To be fair, Arcturus also found that Jev's probabilities aren't always right.

## The numbers, and who's already using it

Vercel wrote that Jev was adopted on AI Gateway faster than any other model in its history. Pricing is $42 per billion input tokens. TypeSafe's headline is 193.6x faster and 444.6x cheaper, but only on System One tasks and only on workflows they picked.

Meanwhile, Ollaya shipped something like Ollama for open models that make these kinds of decisions. Everything runs locally and speaks TypeSafe's API. They tested on 400 states and 2,000 questions:

| Model | Accuracy | Latency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Jev (hosted API) | 0.738 | 236ms–276ms |
| winnow:e4b | 0.722 | 89ms |
| winnow:12b | 0.702 | 131ms |
| laya:en | 0.361 | 10ms |

Note that Jev's latency includes the network, while the others were measured on a local RTX 4090. So this is an order-of-magnitude comparison, not a clean benchmark. Still, an open model hitting 0.722 against 0.738 a week after launch doesn't look good for the moat.

That's also Arcturus's thesis: if OpenAI manages to reproduce the training, it will copy Jev fast and fold the capability into its agents. In their view, TypeSafe's only moat is the data and the training process.

## When to use it, when not, and what to try this week

Don't use it when you need text, when you need long reasoning, or when you can't close the answer space in advance. Do use it for classification, routing, triage and gating. Those are the pipeline steps where today you call an LLM, ask for "yes or no", and hope it doesn't add a sentence of explanation.

What to try this week: take one classification step from your pipeline and 100 labeled examples from real data. Run the 25-line version on them, then check how often the model was right across all the answers that came back at around 0.9. If it's about 90%, you don't need Jev. If it's 60%, that's exactly what TypeSafe is selling.

The idea costs 25 lines. The calibration is the product.
