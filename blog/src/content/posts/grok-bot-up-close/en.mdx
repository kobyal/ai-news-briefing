One computer. That's the most important number in the Grok Bot docs. It matters more than the $20 a month and more than the $300 for SuperGrok Heavy. Every bot in your account works on the same Linux machine in the cloud, and you can put up to six of them in one thread. They share the same files, the same browser sessions and the same CLI credentials.

SpaceXAI, formerly xAI, launched Grok Bot in beta on August 11, 2026. In the seven weeks since, it has shipped an X integration (August 29), an Enterprise version (September 3), a post about a bot that found more than $100,000 in procurement savings (September 4) and Team Bots (September 28). That's a fast pace. The cost is that half the guides online are already out of date. We'll get to that.

In the previous part we looked at Codex, a tool that lives next to the code. Grok Bot goes in a different direction. It doesn't touch the repo. It touches Zendesk, Gmail and the CRM.

## A bot with its own computer, not a chat with buttons

Grok Bot is an agent with a name, a role and memory, running on a VM in SpaceXAI's cloud. It has a browser, a filesystem and a terminal. You sign in to your tools once from inside its computer, and from then on it works alone. It fills out forms, answers email, updates the CRM, and comes back to you when it needs approval. You can give it routines on a schedule, or show it a workflow once. It saves that and runs it on its own next time.

Here's a detail almost nobody mentions: you download it from Cursor, not from xAI. The installer sits on downloads.cursor.com, you log in with Sign In with Cursor, and billing shows up in the Cursor dashboard.

Here's what it isn't:

- **It's not Grok on X.** The chat inside X and the @grok that replies to you in threads are a different product. On August 29 SpaceXAI announced a "tighter integration with X" for the Bot, without saying what that means. When someone says "Grok bot" to you, ask which one they mean.
- **It's not an API.** The Bot has no endpoint. Developers get Grok 4.7 in the API: $2 input and $6 output per million tokens, with a 500k context.
- **It's not fully autonomous.** It stops for approvals, and some sites block it or throw CAPTCHAs at it.

## What happens when you give it work

A disclosure first: we didn't run this ourselves. 4Geeks downloaded all 14 pages of the docs (1,889 lines), and even they wrote that they couldn't test the product itself. What follows comes from the docs and from three people who did work with it.

The flow is simple. You create a bot, give it a name and a role, and message it like you would a teammate. The example on the product page is "Sign in to Zendesk so I can work the support queue". You sign in to the account inside its computer, and it takes over the queue. Want to see what it's doing? You open its screen and watch, or you take control.

Composio ran four bots with separate roles that handed work to each other in a shared thread. A group can hold two to six bots, and they pass ownership of a task without you copying context by hand.

Matthew Granda wrote on September 6 about how he runs a whole fleet. He reached the same conclusion everyone who runs several agents reaches: chat doesn't scale. He talks to only one bot, a Chief of Staff. Under it are bots that each own an area (home, work, writing, Notion), and under those are one-off temps that get deleted when they're done. Notion is the source of record. His best rule: no findings, no ping.

This hierarchy doesn't ship with the product. He built it on top, because without it he was living in the sidebar.

## The numbers, and the sources disagree

The product page today shows three plans. $20 a month through Cursor Pro, with your own computer, routines and weekly usage included. $30 for SuperGrok, with Bot access and Grok 4.6. And $40 per user per month for Cursor Teams Standard.

But 4Geeks, who checked on August 12 and again on August 20, wrote that it isn't sold separately. They said it only comes with Cursor Ultra at $200, SuperGrok Heavy at $300 and Cursor Premium Teams at $120 per user. Composio (August 20) also mentions only Heavy, Ultra and team plans. Yet the launch post itself, on August 11, lists SuperGrok and Cursor Pro too.

Our read: the launch started on the expensive plans and dropped to $20 within a few weeks. That's a read, not a fact. Before you commit, check the pricing page on the day you buy.

One thing is clear: Bot usage is counted separately from your Grok or Cursor quota. A task you hand to the Bot doesn't eat into your Cursor usage.

The sources are just as confused about platforms:

| Source | Date | Desktop | Mobile |
|---|---|---|---|
| Launch post | Aug 11, 2026 | Not specified | iOS |
| Composio | Aug 20, 2026 | macOS, Windows | iPhone only |
| The docs (via 4Geeks) | Sep 2, 2026 | macOS, Windows, Linux | iOS 18+, Android 9+ |

No source lists iPad support. As for the model, the SuperGrok plan lists Grok 4.6, and Grok 4.7 came out on September 21. The pages don't say clearly which one runs your bot today.

## Where it's actually strong

The clearest strength is sites with no API and no MCP. Every company has some 2014 vendor portal nobody will ever write a connector for. A bot that logs in with a username and password and clicks buttons solves exactly that. Roman from the product team put it best in the launch post:

> There is a huge difference between 90% done and 100% done. Most AI gets you almost there. Grok Bot can finish the swing, because the work lands where a human would put it, in the actual tool.
>
> — Roman, Product, SpaceXAI

What he doesn't say there: SpaceXAI itself recommends a connector when one exists, because it's more reliable. Computer use is the fallback, not the main path.

Second, it handles several accounts on one bot. Claire Vo said on How I AI that she has four email addresses and seven Slack workspaces, all connected to a single bot. In her view, Codex and Claude can't do that yet.

Third, setup is fast. You don't build a workflow up front, you just send a message. At SpaceXAI it looks like this: a sales bot that updates the CRM from call transcripts, an ops bot that processes invoices from Gmail, and an engineering bot that reproduces a UI bug, opens a ticket and hands it to a debugging bot. Those are their numbers, on their workflows.

## Where it falls down: everyone on one machine

Back to the number from the top. One computer per user, shared by every bot. The bot that writes your posts sees the same open Gmail session the invoice bot works in, and the same CLI credentials. João Queirós summed it up in one line: "Convenience is the product advantage; permission design is the operating responsibility."

Now think about prompt injection. A bot that reads your inbox also reads email from strangers. If one of those emails convinces it to do something, there's no wall between it and every other account you connected.

> **My take:** We wouldn't give any bot in this account write access to a financial system as long as they all share a machine. It's not a bug. It's a product decision that makes setup fast. But its blast radius is the whole account.

Our daily pipeline has run about ten agents since May 2026. The worst bugs we've had weren't wrong answers. They were silent failures, like an agent that reported success and shipped an empty briefing. A bot that "only comes back when it needs approval" sounds great, until it doesn't come back because it's stuck. Before you hand it real work, ask first: how will I know it failed?

A few more:

- **The Cursor dependency.** Auth, billing and data settings all go through Cursor. That's one more vendor in the chain.
- **Too simple.** You can't pick a model or shape a personality. Claire said her OpenClaw is chaotic and needs maintenance, and that's why she likes it. Grok Bot gives her nothing to tinker with.
- **"24/7" is marketing.** In practice it works 24/7 until the next approval or the next CAPTCHA.
- **The $100,000 in procurement.** Nice. It's a vendor post, on vendor data.

## Versus Errand and OpenClaw

On September 22 Runta published Errand, an open-source alternative to Grok Bot. One person built the first version in a week. It's a desktop app of about 4,000 lines of TypeScript on top of their runtime. Every teammate gets its own isolated machine, with a shell, a browser and a filesystem that persists between tasks. The agent loop is Pi Agent by default, and you can swap it. The live screen is VNC, in 72 lines.

The real difference isn't price. It's isolation. In Errand every teammate has its own machine. In Grok Bot they all share one. In return, Grok Bot gives you a working bot in minutes, a phone app and connectors that already work, with no infrastructure to maintain.

OpenClaw sits at the other end: flexible, chaotic, high-maintenance. Then there's what we covered in the previous part, Codex and Claude Code, which are agents for code, not operations. If your task is a PR, you're in the wrong tool.

## Who should pick this up this quarter

If you're already on Cursor Teams or SuperGrok, yes, try it. The experiment is cheap, and usage doesn't come out of your existing quota. Start the way João recommends: one bot, read-only, with output you can check. A good first job is a morning briefing from your calendar and email that stops before it sends anything. Run it manually twice, save it as a skill, and only then put it on a schedule.

If you're in ops, sales or support, with lots of SaaS and no APIs, this was built for you.

If you're at a regulated company like a bank, an insurer or a health provider, get ready for a talk with security. One VM in a foreign cloud holding sessions for every system, through a Cursor account, is not a short conversation. We haven't yet seen an Israeli team say publicly that it passed a review like that. If you have, we want to hear about it.

And if you want something to write code for you, go back to the previous part.

Let the first bot only read. As long as there's one computer, its permissions are everyone's permissions.
